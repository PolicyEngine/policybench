## 1. The law: which fiscal year funds which tax year

### 1a. C.R.S. 39-22-2003(2), the condition

Source: colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-22-2003, 2024 edition "current through Fall 2025". Fetched 2026-10-06. Pre-freeze statute, in force for 2026. FindLaw's copy, current through 2025-01-01 and reflecting SB24-228, has the same operative clause.

> "With respect to the taxable year commencing on January 1, 1999, and ending December 31, 1999, and for each subsequent taxable year, there shall be allowed to each qualified individual a state sales tax refund in an amount specified in subsection (3) of this section to be claimed in the manner specified in subsection (4) of this section **if there were excess state revenues for the fiscal year ending in that tax year** that voters statewide have not authorized the state to retain and spend and that are required to be refunded pursuant to section 20 (7)(d) of article X of the state constitution."

Colorado's state fiscal year runs July 1 to June 30. FY 2025-26 ends on 2026-06-30, which falls inside tax year 2026. So **the tax year 2026 refund exists only if FY 2025-26 had excess state revenues.** The adversary's mapping is correct.

### 1b. C.R.S. 39-22-2002(2)(a) and (3): the amount and the net-of-other-mechanisms rule

Source: colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-22-2002, current through Fall 2025, and FindLaw, current through 2025-01-01. Both fetched 2026-10-06. Pre-freeze.

- (2)(a): "For the taxable year commencing on or after January 1 of the calendar year in which that fiscal year ended, but prior to January 1 of the subsequent calendar year, the executive director shall divide the total amount of excess state revenues **that is not refunded by another method established by law** and is required to be refunded by the number of qualified individuals expected to claim a refund …"
- (3), as FindLaw renders it: "'excess state revenues' means the total amount of the state revenues for the state fiscal year in excess of the limitation on state fiscal year spending imposed by section 20(7)(a) of article X … and that are not refunded by another method established by law."

This confirms the mapping a second way: a fiscal year ending in calendar 2026 funds the tax year beginning 2026-01-01. Each year's amount is computed afresh as that year's net surplus divided by the expected claimants. Nothing in the statute carries one year's tier amounts into another year.

### 1c. C.R.S. 39-3-209: homestead reimbursement absorbs the surplus first

Source: colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-3-209, current through Fall 2025. Fetched 2026-10-06. Pre-freeze.

> "the lesser of all reimbursement paid by the state treasurer to each treasurer as required by section 39-3-207 (4) for the property tax year that commenced during the state fiscal year or an amount of such reimbursement equal to the amount of excess state revenues for the state fiscal year that are required to be refunded **is a refund of such excess state revenues**."

Read with 39-22-2002(2)(a), which divides only the excess "not refunded by another method established by law", this means the six-tier refund gets only the surplus left after the homestead-exemption reimbursement.

- Deduced from the two texts: no single section states the priority in words. But reimbursement up to the whole excess "is a refund", and the six-tier pool is what "is not refunded by another method", so the reimbursement comes first.
- Corroborated by LCS (June 2026, PDF p. 29): "The first ongoing refund mechanism that applies for each year in the forecast period is the local government reimbursement for homestead property tax exemptions."
- Corroborated by the HB26-1419 fiscal note (1e below). In its scenario where FY 2025-26 exceeds the cap by up to $202.2M, the extra refund goes "via property tax reimbursements to local governments", with $0 to six-tier.

LCS June 2026 put the FY 2025-26 reimbursement at $199.2M (homestead) plus $4.1M (SB24-111). **A tax year 2026 six-tier refund therefore needed FY 2025-26 revenue more than about $203M above the cap**, not merely above it.

### 1d. Legislation through the freeze changing the mechanism: none for the six-tier refund

The LCS June 2026 forecast incorporates "all other 2026 legislation" (p. 3). It lists the mechanisms in force: "Current state law includes two ongoing refund mechanisms for FY 2025-26 and beyond [homestead reimbursement; six-tier sales tax refund]. There are additionally two refund mechanisms established in Senate Bill 24-228 … and one additional temporary refund mechanism established in Senate Bill 24-111 for FY 2024-25 and FY 2025-26" (PDF p. 29). No 2025 or 2026 act changed the six-tier mechanism or its surplus condition.

The 2026 acts that touch TABOR accounting are:

- **HB26-1419**, signed 2026-06-03 (chapter 369), contingent on there being no FY 2025-26 surplus.
- **HB26-1364**, which changes the inflation used in the limit for FY 2026-27 and 2027-28.
- **SB26-042**, which reclassifies certain revenue.

### 1e. HB26-1419, Over-Refund of Excess State Revenues

- **Bill page** (leg.colorado.gov/bills/HB26-1419): signed and became law 2026-06-03. It operates "if the state controller certifies in September 2026 that state revenues for state fiscal year 2025-26 did not exceed the limitation". It is premised on a likely shortfall but does not itself establish one.
- **Fiscal note**, first revised note, dated 2026-05-01: "If the state collects a TABOR surplus in FY 2025-26, as identified by the State Controller in the September 1, 2026, TABOR revenue certification for FY 2025-26, then the bill's substantive provisions have no effect." It also says: "Under the March 2026 OSPB forecast used by the Joint Budget Committee (JBC) for budget balancing, FY 2025-26 revenue could exceed the Referendum C cap by up to $202.2 million." In that scenario its Table 1B sends the refund through property tax reimbursements, with six-tier refunds of "$0" for FY 2026-27.

## 4. Decision and the freeze rule

### 4a. The benchmark's freeze rule, quoted

- `docs/benchmark_card.md:79-85`: "A scored reference follows from the stated facts and from law published before PolicyBench froze the references on 2026-07-03. Where policyengine-us projects a 2026 amount with a price index, or carries one published after the freeze, the reference takes the amount published before the freeze or, where none was, the last one published. Nine publication conventions set those amounts, and each changes parameter values only."
- `reference_audit/2026-09-22/README.md:7`: "Where the engine projected a 2026 parameter with a price index, a publication convention regenerates the reference with the amount published before the freeze, or with the last amount published. A convention changes parameter values only."
- `paper/index.qmd:1079`: "Where no 2026 amount published before the freeze was found, the reference takes the last amount published: California's indexed amounts, whose 2026 factor rests on June 2026 prices; the IRS optional sales tax tables …; Idaho's zero-rate threshold; Maryland's return deduction; and SNAP's FY2027 figures, published on 2026-08-21."

### 4b. Application

1. **The publication convention does not reach this parameter by its terms.** It is triggered where the engine "projects a 2026 amount with a price index, or carries one published after the freeze". The CO tier table is neither. It is a 2025 entry carried forward, so the reference stands or falls as the engine's encoding of pre-freeze law.
2. **As an encoding of pre-freeze law it is wrong.** The statute (1a, 1b) makes each tax year's refund conditional on, and computed from, that tax year's fiscal-year surplus net of homestead reimbursement. Carrying the tax year 2025 amounts forward asserts two things no law says: that FY 2025-26 had a surplus above about $203M, and that this surplus per claimant reproduced FY 2024-25's tier amounts exactly.
3. **Even if the hold-last-published convention were extended by analogy** (the California parallel: a factor resting on a pre-freeze-period fact published after the freeze), the "amount published before the freeze" for **tax year 2026** exists. LCS published a tax year 2026 six-tier table three times before the freeze (December, March, June), each $0 in every tier, and OSPB said "no refunds." The $19 table is the published amount for **tax year 2025**, a different year's surplus distribution. Holding a California bracket at its 2025 value approximates the 2026 law to within inflation. Holding the 2025 refund table puts the household on the branch of the statute that every official source said would not occur.
4. **What did rest on a forecast.** At the freeze, FY 2025-26 had closed (2026-06-30), but it was not certified until 2026-09-08. The $0 therefore rested on official forecasts at the freeze, not on a certification. Those forecasts were unanimous for the six-tier refund: LCS −$424.9M; OSPB −$15.3M, whose own upside case still gives no six-tier refund. The post-freeze certification (−$175.9M) confirmed them.
5. **Neither disposition supports $19.**
   - (a) Regenerate to **$0**. This follows the statute plus every pre-freeze official tax year 2026 publication, and matches the certified outcome. This is my recommendation.
   - (b) If the maintainer rules that a value which rested on a forecast at the freeze cannot be scored, **exclude** the output. On that reading the pre-freeze answer is undetermined.

   Under no reading of the law or the facts is $19 the answer. That is why I class this CONFIRMED rather than AMBIGUOUS. The open question is only how to handle the cell ($0 or exclusion), not whether $19 survives.

In `parameters/gov/states/co/tax/income/credits/sales_tax_refund/amount/scale.yaml`, add `2026-01-01: 0` to each of the six `amount` series. Add these references:

### 6b. Structural fix upstream (preferred)

Add a gate so the engine stops paying a refund in any year that has not been certified:

- New parameter `gov/states/co/tax/income/credits/sales_tax_refund/in_effect.yaml`: `2021-01-01: true`, `2026-01-01: false`. - In `co_sales_tax_refund.py`, return `eligible_count * amount * p.in_effect`.

Maintainers then flip it to `true` and add a new tier row when CDOR publishes each year's table. LCS September 2026 projects a tax year 2027 refund, $43 at the lowest single tier. Until certified, carrying `false` into 2027 is the forecast-free default.
