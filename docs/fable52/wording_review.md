# Review of the judge's texts for release dashboard-data-20261111

This page records a reading of the judge's texts for release dashboard-data-20261111, which adds Claude Fable 5.2 as the board's 52nd model, and what the reading found. The session that operated the release runs on Claude Fable 5.2, so PolicyBench had other models read the texts and made no discretionary change to references or scoring on their findings. Claude Opus 5.5, the judge's own model, read the cells Claude Fable 5.2 answered wrong, and GPT-6.1 Sol read the cells it answered right.

## What was reviewed and by whom

PolicyBench scores models' answers to questions on 100 households in tax year 2026 against references computed by policyengine-us 2.60.0. For every cell (one household and one output) that some model answered wrong, the judge, Claude Opus 5.5, writes a case note and a sentence for each wrong row, each with a class. For this release the judge wrote new texts for the 122 cells Claude Fable 5.2 answered wrong, and they replace the published texts for every model in those cells.

Two reads covered the judged cells.

- **First read, 2026-11-11.** Six Claude Opus 5.5 readers, the same model as the judge, split the 122 re-judged cells. Each case file held the reference, any decision on record, the notes of release 20261027 and of this release, and every new row with its answer and class, with Claude Fable 5.2's rows marked. The readers checked for unanswered doubts about the reference or the prompt, the law each diagnosis rests on, the fit of each class to its text, equal treatment of Claude Fable 5.2's rows, and hygiene. Their brief set the law as tax year 2026 as enacted by 2026-07-03 and said the engine's own conventions are not errors.
- **Second read, 2026-11-11.** GPT-6.1 Sol readers read, in 14 batches, the 559 judged cells Claude Fable 5.2 answered right, where it has no row, with the same checks less the row comparison. Their brief added three conventions: a 2026 amount not published by 2026-07-03 takes the last one published; the prompt's rule that an unlisted fact is false sits beside PolicyBench's exclusions of cells where "a careful reader could take the stated facts the other way"; and a yes-or-no output asks what PolicyEngine returns under its definition. The brief also said answers match to the cent. That was wrong: the scorer counts an amount as right within $1 ([benchmark card](../benchmark_card.md)).

Readers in both reads checked legal facts against primary sources (statutes, regulations, agency pages, enrolled bills) under caps of 3 lookups a case and 25 (first read) or 30 (second read) a batch. The readers of four batches report going over the per-case cap, on six cases in all. The first read used 111 lookups, and the readers of all 14 second-read batches report web access. Neither read saw the prompts. The readers worked from the case files described above, so their doubts about a prompt's completeness are raised from what the judge's texts and the answers show.

The doubts about a reference or a prompt (P1s, defined below) then went to adjudicators, who read each case's prompt and worked from it, the engine's code, the law and PolicyBench's record of decisions, and ran no engine. Their results are provisional inputs to any ruling by PolicyBench, not rulings.

- **Round one, 2026-11-11.** Claude Opus 5.5 adjudicators took 13 causes covering 21 of the first read's 24 P1s, plus one cause from two P3 findings on employer insurance premiums and a saver's credit. A GPT-6.1 Sol reviewer tried to refute each of the 14 and adjudicated two more P1s on its own, for 16 items. The last P1, on SNAP farm rent in scenario_112, concerns an output the release does not score, so no adjudicator took it.
- **Round two, 2026-11-12.** Claude Opus 5.5 adjudicators took the ten second-read batches that hold a P1, and all ten are complete, covering the 21 P1s. The adjudications of the last three batches to finish, which hold 12 of the 21, were written under the scorer's rule that a dollar answer is right within $1; the brief for round one and for the adjudications of the first seven batches stated the cent rule. Under the $1 rule, PolicyBench has rechecked only round one: no round-one class rests on a gap under $1, and every other dollar value round one gives differs from its reference by more than $1. The adjudications of three of the first seven batches set the brief's cent wording aside and applied the $1 rule themselves; those of the other four have not been rechecked. No refutation pass by another lab has run on this round.

Both reviewing models have rows on the board. Claude Opus 5.5 is an Anthropic model, as Claude Fable 5.2 is, and it made the first read, 14 of round one's 16 adjudications, all of round two's and the errata check described below; no model from another lab took part in the first read or the errata check. GPT-6.1 Sol is an OpenAI model. Each round-one prompt asked it to disclose the stake of its own lab, OpenAI, and 12 of its 14 refutations and both of its own adjudications name OpenAI as its lab.

## What this release does with the findings

This release changes no reference and no scored output on these findings. That holds for the four questions the errata check raised on scored cells too, although replacing the reference with the other reading's value in `scenario_026 child1_chip_eligible` or `scenario_100 tanf` would raise Claude Fable 5.2's score, as would $119.38 in `scenario_095 state_refundable_credits`. The section on open questions gives the direction for Claude Fable 5.2 of each question with a stated other value, and they cut both ways. The first read covered only cells Claude Fable 5.2 answered wrong, so acting on it alone would favour that model; the second read covers the cells it answered right, so that the sample runs both ways. PolicyBench has decided none of the open questions for any model and has set no date for deciding them; a decision would draw on both reads together and take effect in a release after this one. Apart from one Minnesota output, `scenario_122 state_refundable_credits`, which PolicyBench decided on 2026-11-01 to stop scoring, the release keeps release 20261027's references and scored set. The judge's texts are published as written. [The judge-text errata](judge_text_errata.md) list the 36 of the first read's 50 findings of false or unsupported statements that the check upheld. The second read's 86 findings of the same level have had no separate check and are not listed; nor are either read's wording and hygiene findings (P3).

The board's app shows each upheld correction to a model's row sentence under the sentence it corrects. The app shows row sentences, not case notes, so corrections to case notes are listed only in the errata. On 2026-11-12, GPT-6.1 Sol reviewed, from a bundle of their text and without access to the repository, this record, the release note, the errata's header with 12 of its entries, the car-loan audit's summary page, the effort sensitivity's plan, code change, note and summary data, and two parts of the release's design note, the disclosures it requires of the release note and its completion record. It asked for changes before publication. In response, this version states what the readers saw, what was rechecked under the $1 rule, the standard the errata check applied to the four wording findings and the conditions on the Oklahoma SNAP reason, and it adds the 403(b) question in `scenario_023 ssi`. The further checks it asked for have not run, among them a check of the second read's 86 findings and a review of the four wording findings under the equal-treatment standard.

## The car-loan pattern

In 21 households the prompt lists auto loan interest above zero and states nothing about the vehicle or the loan. The federal deduction for qualified passenger vehicle loan interest (26 U.S.C. 163(h)(4), added by P.L. 119-21 for tax years 2025 to 2028) covers interest on a loan taken out after 2024 to buy a new vehicle for personal use, with final assembly in the United States and a gross vehicle weight rating under 14,000 pounds, secured by a first lien. The engine allows the deduction only from a separate input for qualifying interest that no scenario sets, so every reference denies it. One reading applies the prompt's rule that "any other unlisted household fact, boolean, or status input" is false, and keeps the references. The other takes the listing as a statement that the loan qualifies, since, as a first-read reader noted, the line has no other use on a federal return. The round-one adjudication answered that the prompt lists every nonzero input the engine receives, so a listed line does not signal that it matters, and the GPT-6.1 Sol refutation answered that listing an expense does not establish that it is deductible. The adjudication still found that a careful reader could take "auto loan interest" in a 2026 federal tax prompt as the household's deductible car-loan interest, much as "home mortgage interest" reads as qualified residence interest, in the engine too. The Claude Opus 5.5 adjudication and the GPT-6.1 Sol refutation both class the pattern as a prompt that leaves a needed fact open.

Entering each household's stated interest as qualifying interest and rerunning policyengine-us 2.60.0 moves 15 cells by more than $1. Eleven are scored: `federal_income_tax_before_refundable_credits` in scenarios 003, 018, 053, 067, 086, 099 and 101; `federal_refundable_credits` in 026 and 028; and `state_income_tax_before_refundable_credits` in 053 (Idaho) and 067 (Indiana). The other four (federal income tax in 002, 033 and 093, local income tax in 067) are already unscored and stay so below. In the 11 scored cells, 81 model answers match only the reference and 22 only the other reading's value; Claude Fable 5.2 matches the other reading in 7 and the reference in 1.

Each scoring uses PolicyBench's headline score: within each household, the weighted share of scored outputs a model answered right (an amount within $1 of the reference, a yes-or-no output exactly), averaged over the 100 households with equal weight, with output weights taken from population data.
